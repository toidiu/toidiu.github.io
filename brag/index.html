# Work samples

## Proxy H3 Migration

I led a complex and high-risk production migration of CDN traffic at Cloudflare. The project was challenging because it required significant effort to align a diverse set of stakeholders while ensuring the technical decisions were sound. The goal was to migrate 4.5M+ unique customers (25M+ req/sec) from nginx to the new Rust proxy. With stakeholders spanning directors, PMs, customer support, and engineers across the organization, I had to understand our system both at the technical and operational level. I started by developing a deep understanding of how our proxy integrated with the networking infrastructure at Cloudflare (Addressing, DNS, and load-balancing). For example, this included understanding how the IP Addressing team routed traffic for different customer tiers and how they load-balanced requests across different processes.

At multiple stages I explored various technical solutions, weighing the “soft” vs “hard” requirements for different stakeholders while building alignment. For example, due to customer requirements, it was not possible to change IPs for existing customers. Since IPs could only be assigned to a single proxy process, this meant customer migration would require migrating IPs between the old and new proxy. Critically, this meant we could not write clean logic around customer IDs and would instead need to integrate with the Addressing team to migrate IPs. While IP stability was critical to a successful migration from the customer's perspective, it introduced major technical challenges. Since most of the 4.5M+ customers had single-address prefixes (/32 and /128), this means I would have to create batches from 4.5M IPs while considering customer tier, blast radius, and rollback status. I was able to gain consensus for this design by drafting several proposals and asking stakeholders to flag "hard" blockers. This helped surface non-viable solutions early and kept the project moving.

Once we had rough consensus on a technical solution, I began drafting a detailed specification of the migration plan. Because this was such a large and dynamic project, my goal was to create a living document and iteratively build toward the final plan. I completed the first draft within a week and set the plan in motion. I got initial feedback from my principal engineer to confirm the general engineering direction made sense. Then I expanded the review to the entire team, the PM, and my skip-level manager. Importantly, throughout this entire process I captured all feedback as outstanding "TODO(owner)" items, assigning each to the correct owner (usually myself). This approach added visibility into all decisions and let me keep the momentum going.

At 4.5M IPs, I very quickly realized the need for a software-based solution (migration-utility) that could manage the migration state, add guardrails, and most importantly act as the single source of truth. The migration-utility turned out to be critical for building trust. It enabled safe migration operations (progress, rollback) for the entire on-call team and gave customer-support teams visibility into the migration state. I was also able to build trust with the Addressing team by building a "staging" feature flag in the migration-utility and executing test migrations against the staging version of the Addressing database.

The migration was approved for execution but unfortunately I was impacted by layoffs just before I could execute it. I am unaware of the current migration status but believe my work should be reusable since I wrote a detailed specification outlining and justifying the plan, an Engineering SOP, a Customer Support SOP and a migration-utility that automates the dangerous part of the migration.

## Invalid State Error

https://github.com/cloudflare/quiche/pull/2366

While developing the new Rust proxy at Cloudflare, one of my coworkers noticed an increase in the number of InvalidState errors. The InvalidState error was a generic catch-all emitted when a quiche connection was in a bad state. We expected a non-zero number of errors since CDN traffic contains malformed or malicious clients, but an increase in the number of errors needed investigation. Attempts to find the root cause were not successful and we filed a ticket as we continued to work on other critical components. I picked up the ticket almost a year later, as we neared the launch date.

The main challenge with this investigation was the limited telemetry and the use of loosely typed errors. Since the proxy served a high volume of traffic, any increase in logging could be dangerous to the logging infrastructure (and amplified by a DDoS attack). I felt this pain as my first attempt to annotate errors with additional context resulted in a behavior change to the connection close reason and an increase in log volume. The change was reverted and I had to rethink my strategy.

The original goal of contextualizing the errors was to segment them without blowing up the logging pipeline. However, since what I really needed were detailed logs, I proposed switching to a "staging" build (deployed on ~10 bare-metal servers). With the build isolated to a smaller fleet but still being cautious not to overload the pipeline, I iteratively added more logs and traced the various sources of the InvalidState error. Most of the error sources were reasonable and stemmed from handling malformed client payloads (e.g. invalid CID).

A few iterations later I hit an interesting log line that roughly looked like: "paths: 2, active_path: x". This "smelled" funny because all these errors were happening in the presence of '2' paths, but path migration is not a common feature. Having previously implemented connection migration in s2n-quic, I quickly deduced that NAT rebinds can cause passive path migration independent of the client implementation. The error occurred when the PMTU probing logic (a performance optimization) attempted to access a non-existent active path after the migration. My proposed fix was to skip PMTU probes in these cases and avoid closing the connection.

Once fixed, the logs showed an 80% reduction in InvalidState errors. Although the PMTU bug affected only 0.17% of the total proxy traffic, that amounted to roughly a thousand customer connections being torn down every second on the new proxy. Given that quiche was used across other services, the real impact was much larger.

## Go Proxy TLS hangs

While on-call at Cloudflare, I was paged for an incident where customers were experiencing failures when trying to establish new connections to the Go proxy. Existing connections continued to work fine and the only useful information in the core dump was a long-running goroutine, whose stack trace pointed at a specific source file.

While technically owned by our team, the Go proxy was in unofficial "maintenance" mode since all the original authors were on different teams. Even though my weekly on-call shift was coming to an end, I decided to pick up the investigation because our team needed to own our software, there was real customer impact, and this would be a fantastic opportunity to learn Go.

I started with the source file mentioned in the core dump and focused on a complex loop which was using locks to make RPC calls and establish a new connection on failure. I started to suspect a deadlock, but being new to Go, I first had to confirm this by investigating the library internals. In my investigation into the RPC and new connection calls, I did not find any timeouts, which deepened my suspicion. The fix later confirmed this: a goroutine establishing a new connection would hang, never release the lock, and block all new connections.

Pursuing the deadlock theory, I started by adding observability to try and detect the issue and also measure the customer impact. However, a deadlock is tricky to observe: I was trying to measure a lack of metrics, which is hard to differentiate from low traffic volume. I ended up adding histogram metrics to measure the distribution of time spent in the RPC loop, and collaborated with the original authors to estimate the current impact (~0.2% of the fleet, or 200 proxy instances, stuck at any given time).

Next, I prepared a small patch to add timeouts to both the RPC and new connection calls. Unfortunately, this was blocked due to a multi-month production freeze. Motivated by the facts that this was likely the fix, that real customers were impacted, and that a long freeze would not be acceptable, I set out to convince my manager and leadership. I wrote a concise document describing the problem, my investigation, estimated customer impact, why the proposed fix was urgent, and why the fix was low risk. I was able to get approvals up the leadership chain (up to director), and I confirmed the fix via the histogram metrics once the patch was released.

## QUIC OACK Security Vulnerability

Two months into the Protocols role at Cloudflare, I was tasked with patching a security vulnerability in our QUIC library. At the same time, my two principal engineers had planned vacation for two weeks, so I would need to make progress by myself.

Protocol-level vulnerabilities are notoriously difficult to debug: implementations tend to be RFC-compliant, but reproducing a security issue requires a deliberately non-compliant counterpart. Reproduction therefore requires a deep understanding of the RFC and codebase to implement the non-compliant behavior.

I owned the full remediation lifecycle: reproducing the issue, implementing the fix, patching the proxy, and coordinating patches across three other infrastructure teams. ## Session Resumption

One of the most technically challenging projects I undertook was adding session resumption and 0-RTT to our new Rust proxy at Cloudflare. The project was particularly challenging because it was my first project in the new role, in addition to the complicated nature of session resumption.

The high-level goal of session resumption is to cache session state in order to skip some expensive aspects of the full TLS handshake for subsequent connections. The savings come with protocol and API complexity, so it's even more important to design resilient APIs that are easy to audit and hard to misuse.

I started by deeply understanding the session resumption feature, studying how nginx used it, and writing a specification based on my previous experience with protocol design. Since the entire stack was new to me, I relied on my team to review my specification and surface legacy decisions that needed to be ported from the old proxy.

The first tricky technical decision was handling rotating session ticket keys based on a TTL. This logic needed to be resilient because of all the potential edge cases due to missing keys. I had to handle loading keys asynchronously, generating session ticket keys from secrets, and fallback logic to deal with missing keys (empty or partially missing key set). Testing this logic was quite tricky because it was difficult to isolate the rotation logic from the session ticket caching layer.

The most technically difficult aspect of the project was understanding the BoringSSL API and exposing Rust bindings for it. Crossing the FFI boundary makes development much riskier because you are dealing with unsafe boundaries that can panic, or worse, fail silently. Considering that we were building a secure proxy, a bug could have serious implications. I implemented a safe Rust interface for session resumption, added tests, and made sure to document the implicit C contract. While I could have used the unsafe low-level C bindings directly, the safe bindings enabled cleaner control flow and made the code easier to test and instrument.

Integrating session resumption was simple compared with testing the feature. Most TLS features tend to be stateless, so adding testing support for session resumption involved diving into the entire stack, five layers deep (BoringSSL, quiche, tokio-quiche, oxy framework, Rust proxy).

Implementing 0-RTT didn't require much code but demanded deep investigation of the C logic in BoringSSL. The TLS state machine during a 0-RTT resumption is complicated since it bypasses many of the procedures of a full handshake and instead derives 0-RTT keys from the session ticket. I had to build a comprehensive model of TLS, 0-RTT, QUIC and all the proxy layers in between to get the feature working.

I pride myself on creating systems that are simple, and a large part of that is exposing rich metrics. I am especially proud of the observability I added for the session resumption feature. The Cloudflare proxy serves millions of req/sec (more during a DDoS attack), so good observability is critical to sane operation. I added POP-specific metrics for session ticket keys and resumption status. The session ticket key metrics tracked key expiration and the use of fallback keys, and were useful for detecting infrastructure issues. The resumption status metrics were useful for detecting customer issues and finding gaps in our resumption design. Based on these metrics, I estimated that it was possible to further increase the resumption rate by up to 15% compared to the current global rate. I wrote an internal blog post suggesting the improvement and estimating the effort.

## QUIC ACK frequency

Tracking issue: https://github.com/aws/s2n-quic/issues/1276 Final PR to enable delayed ACKs: https://github.com/aws/s2n-quic/pull/1298

The project that best reflects "good engineering" for me was implementing the experimental ACK frequency RFC in s2n-quic at AWS. Based on flamegraph analysis, ACK processing was an expensive part of the QUIC stack, and the motivation behind the work was to reduce the ACK frequency to make the protocol more efficient. The RFC proposed a custom protocol extension which required peer negotiation and could result in less frequent ACK processing. I started by understanding the RFC and writing a specification for team review.

The first proposal was to implement the full RFC, but this had many drawbacks. Since the extension required peer negotiation and the RFC was experimental, it was unlikely that any peer would support the feature. Additionally, tuning the frequency of ACKs would be non-trivial and would likely depend on customer workload. A lower frequency of ACKs would also impact Congestion Control and Loss Recovery in unpredictable ways, potentially harming connection stability. High implementation effort and low reward meant this was the least desirable option.

The second proposal was to batch ACK information on the receive path across multiple GSO payloads and process it in bulk: Receive path (multi-round). The theory was that bulk processing would mimic the benefits of less frequent ACK delivery. Instead of negotiating the ACK frequency with the peer, the local implementation would queue the ACK information from received packets across multiple GSO payloads (tunable) and process it once. We suspected that the expensive part in the receive path was the context-switching required for each QUIC packet. The benefit of this approach was the lack of extension negotiation, which meant all connections could benefit from the work. The risk to Congestion Control and Loss Recovery remained, however.

The third proposal, and my recommendation, was to batch ACK information across packets in a single GSO payload: Receive path (single-round). Each GSO payload is essentially a collection of QUIC packets (~10) that are transmitted and received together. The default implementation processed the ACK information in each packet individually, and the insight was that we could extract CPU savings by first queuing and then processing it once per payload. The benefits over the multi-round approach were that CloudFront would not need to tune their systems and there would be no impact on Congestion Control and Loss Recovery. Another benefit was that the work would naturally extend to the more sophisticated proposals above.

After the team agreed with my recommendation, I started to implement the Receive path (single-round) proposal. The work was broken down into small atomic PRs that exposed queuing and delayed packet processing on the receive path. The final PR was simple and mostly enabled the feature.

However, to my surprise, the new code actually performed worse in CI tests, regressing from 24.33% to 25.81% of CPU time in flamegraph analysis. I investigated the regression by running download simulations with different payload sizes and confirmed there were no performance improvements. Backed by data, we decided to abandon the feature. My theory is that queuing ACK information was more expensive than processing it right away in s2n-quic.

While the project was not a success in the typical sense, it demonstrated the rigorous engineering process and testing that caught the regression before it was merged. I also suspect that ACK frequency would be more beneficial in a data center environment where network links are faster, peer negotiation is possible, and there is less loss on the network.

## Meetup: Sans I/O

https://github.com/toidiu/raft-rs/blob/main/sansio_presentation.pdf

In this presentation, I wanted to share lessons learned while building sans I/O protocol implementations. sans I/O advocates for implementing the core protocol logic as synchronous code and avoiding all use of I/O (async, timers, socket, etc.). The I/O then gets "bolted" on top of the protocol logic. The primary benefit of this is maintainable code, robust error handling, and testing at the protocol layer. Additionally, the I/O layer becomes swappable and can easily integrate with various language runtimes.

I wanted to share this because while sans I/O is specific to protocols, it should be possible to use this design for other software. Finally, I feel even though async is powerful, it can lead to complicated code (channel hell) that is hard to reason about if not careful.

## What work have you done that you are particularly proud of and why?

I am proud of my work in launching a new EC2 Fingerprint service at AWS. The service replaced core legacy logic which was used to determine EC2 instance compatibility. I am especially proud because the migration to the new service went smoothly due to the extensive testing and observability I added.

My team owned an orchestrator which consumed the compatibility rules from a DynamoDB table owned by the hardware team. Rules were frequently updated by the hardware team but would occasionally result in broken metals and customer impact. One of the motivations behind the Fingerprint service was to address this customer impact, scale to support more rules, and act as a single source of truth for EC2 compatibility for the organization.

There were a few reasons why this project was especially risky. First, due to the large number of compatibility rules (1500), a manual audit of the rules was impractical. Second, the hardware team wanted to change the rule format, which meant ensuring parity when switching to the new service was a lot harder. Finally, any mistakes in compatibility meant serious customer impact.

The development work was tricky due to the changing format, and I spent most of my time writing tests. To build confidence, I wrote a test to generate all possible compatibility combinations and compare them between the new and old format. This testing ended up catching a few mistakes, which the hardware team then fixed.

After the testing, I was very confident about the logic, but given the high potential impact for customers, I decided to integrate safely and double-check the logic in production. I integrated the new service into the orchestrator but only enabled it in "observability mode". Each call would generate a compatibility result from both the new and old service and emit a metric if there was a difference. After running for a week without any mismatch, I started the production release. As expected, there was zero customer impact.

## For a pair of values, describe a time in which the two values came into tension for you or your work, and how you resolved it.

Sample values: Candor, Humor, Teamwork, Courage, Optimism, Thriftiness, Curiosity, Resilience, Transparency, Diversity, Responsibility, Urgency, Empathy, Rigor, Versatility

Urgency/Rigor

My transition to the Cloudflare Protocols team from the AWS Transport Libraries team took some calibrating. While both teams work on Protocols, Cloudflare's culture was to move fast compared to AWS's culture. At AWS, we sometimes spent weeks or even months writing detailed specifications and ensuring the design was robust and compatible with all potential use cases. In the protocol and security domain, I was used to working methodically and making sure my work was perfect. It was therefore a bit of a surprise when I got feedback from my manager that I needed to work on adopting Cloudflare's culture of "move fast".

After some thought, I realized that Cloudflare was successful because of its ability to move fast. Drawing from my AWS values, I often pushed for rigorous analysis before shipping features. I had to come to terms with the realization that not all features were equally critical and that I could do more to communicate this distinction.

To resolve this situation, I worked hard on internalizing the "move fast" culture. Rather than simply suggesting rigorous deliverables, I started breaking up requirements based on how critical they were. Specifically, I started to emphasize the importance of the critical components while acknowledging the less critical components and offering solutions to move faster. This eventually helped me earn my manager's trust. In retrospect, the feedback was extremely valuable, and I learned the importance of balancing urgency and rigor in proportion to the criticality of the task.

## For one of the values, describe an example of how it was violated in your organization or work.

Sample values: Candor, Humor, Teamwork, Courage, Optimism, Thriftiness, Curiosity, Resilience, Transparency, Diversity, Responsibility, Urgency, Empathy, Rigor, Versatility

Teamwork/Empathy

In my experience, a high functioning team is made up of members that feel like they can make an impact but also have the psychological safety to fail while learning. This culture was threatened at Cloudflare, as leadership decided to hire five new junior engineers on an existing team of seven. Onboarding is especially difficult in an async/remote team, and this is especially true for junior engineers. The entire situation was exacerbated as three senior-tenure engineers decided to switch teams or leave around the same time.

Having been in a similar situation before, I saw hints of stress as new engineers were expected to start delivering in a deeply technical domain but had very little mentoring. I decided to step in and start mentoring my teammates and ensuring they were successful.

For example, I mentored a teammate via code reviews and 1:1 meetings. Rather than flooding a PR with nits, I limited my feedback to a few items and slowly left more specific feedback over time. I also initiated a few ad hoc 1:1s to discuss design choices and best practices. New engineers can often be shy, so I felt it was important for me to initiate these 1:1s. Over the next few months, the engineer became more independent, and the code quality improved dramatically.

In another example, I just listened as a coworker vented about having to move to a new office location. The move was tough for her due to the lack of friends and family. While I didn't actually solve her problems, I believe listening is an important part of building trust and psychological safety.
